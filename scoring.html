One number, and it can't be argued with.

Every challenge we run produces a single score, counted by software rather than by a person with a clipboard. Here is exactly how that number is built, why it rewards the students who push hardest, and what it means for what a school raises.

The rule

Reach round 12 and you have scored 78 points.

A challenge is a chain that grows by one every round. Round one is a single item. Round two is two. By the time a student holds a chain of twelve, they have correctly recalled 1 + 2 + 3 all the way to 12 — seventy-eight separate acts of memory, in one unbroken run.

The score is not a made-up number attached to a level. It is the actual count of what the student did. That is why it holds up: a parent can be told precisely what their pledge is paying for.

Every round is worth more than the last

This is the part that changes how students behave. Because each round adds one more item to the chain, the points a round is worth go up as the run goes on. The tenth round is worth ten. The twentieth is worth twenty.

Total points at each round. The line is not straight — that is the whole point.

A student who improves from ten rounds to twelve has not improved by twenty percent. They have gone from 55 points to 78 — a forty percent jump in what they raise. The effort at the edge of what they can hold is worth the most, which is exactly where you want a student working.

Why it is built this way

Nobody counts it The software records the run. There is no tally sheet, no volunteer, and nothing for a parent to question. In a fundraiser where money depends on a number, that matters more than anything else about it.
Effort is what moves it A student cannot raise their score by having wealthier relatives. They raise it by holding one more round than last time — which takes practice, and practice is the part that sharpens.
The prize is built in A score that climbs is its own motivation. There is no prize ladder to fund, nothing to order, nothing to hand out, and no student left out because their family could not sell enough.
Nobody scores zero A student who holds three rounds still scores six points, and their sponsors are still invoiced. Every student contributes something, which is not true of most fundraisers.

What it does not do

Worth saying plainly: a student with ten sponsors raises more than a student with two, however well they play. That is true of every pledge fundraiser there has ever been, and we have not solved it.

What the score does is make the second half fair. Once the sponsors are found, what each one pays is decided by the student's own work on the day — not by how many doors they knocked on.
